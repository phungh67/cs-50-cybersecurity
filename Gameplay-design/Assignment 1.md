## Outline

### 1. Supplement:
- Game is a rule-based system in which the goal is for one player to win. So basically, we have the winning condition for this one, and it will stir up the competitive spirit between contestants.
- Puzzles are rule-based systems. They can be very challenging, funny or trivial at all. But the ultimate goal is to find the solution for it. It may or may not have replay value. And the puzzle is somewhat "static"?
- Toys are manipulable, like puzzles (by human) but there is no fixed goal about it.
- Stories are vital for a good game, but cannot be changed or manipulated. Can only be "passed" through people by spreading it.

### 2. Structure or components that a game should have

- Players: yes, should have, because it can create the competitive between human, that leads to a conflict, hence achieves a final winner.
- Objectives: define the winning condition, for example, take all other players chess, take the highest score,...
- Procedures: a finite steps to achieve or to complete the objectives of the game.
- Rules: enforce a set of agreement between participants to control the game, to limit behavior within a game.
- Resources: the items can be used in game, can be value, can be strategic,...
- Conflict: created by the conjunction of procedures, winning conditions, rules and objectives between players.
- Boundaries: the limited space that the game took part-in. For example, the limit of a card game is a specific set of cards that players brought before entering the duel.
- Outcome: the final result, contains winner, loser,...

>[!Definition]
>A game is an encapsulated system with distinct rules, fixed boundaries and limited resources in which participants compete for a defined victory state, leading to an inequality of results.

## Essay

**"A game is an encapsulated system with distinct rules, boundaries, and resources in which participants compete for a defined victory state, leading to an inequality of results."**

The game should be set in its own world so that its result remains separate from our reality. For example, in some role-playing games, two best friends can "harm" each other's characters without any consequences, and the outcome of those "harming" actions affects only that reality. Boundaries and rules are essential because they ensure that all participants' actions are predictable and only aim to achieve the winning conditions. Furthermore, the boundary is set to limit the space of the game, navigating the players' attention to the main goal. Take the example of a chess game: because two players only have a board of 64 squares, they have to calculate, plan, and take each step carefully to not only keep their pieces but also take as many of the opponent's pieces as possible. Lastly, an appropriate game according to the above definition must have a winning condition, hence yielding an inequality of results. The winning condition in this case is very similar to a stop condition in programming; it defines the end state of the game. Once a player or a group successfully acquires all necessary conditions to trigger the victory, the game will stop. Since resources are limited and participants have to compete for them, an inequality is inevitable.

The above definition shares the same point of view about the "limited" context of a game from the definitions of J. Huizinga and C.C. Abt. This limitation is vital for a game to function properly because a limited context will stir conflict between players, allowing them to compete to acquire as many resources as possible. But unlike the idea that a "game is just an activity", nowadays, a game is more like a separate world in terms of time and space. It can also be viewed as a subset of reality because many modern games have their own worlds derived from our world (with some modifications). It also agrees with C. Crawford that the game should possess "safety"; because it is an encapsulated system, every consequence produced during the playing process should not have (or at least have no notable) physical effects on real life. For the winning condition, the definition inherits the idea from G. Costikyan and E. Avedon & B. Sutton-Smith that in-game resources lead to a disequilibrial outcome. Finally, in conjunction with the definition given by T. Fullerton, C. Swain, and S. Hoffman, this definition is more flexible because it does not explicitly require a "closed formal system". Take the example in the multi-players game, where many people can connect or disconnect at will and does not have any notable effect on the playing progress. The said game is not closed, because it accepts new participants, but still limits the context, resources, etc.

In terms of exclusions, this definition intentionally omits two concepts commonly found in classical frameworks: "voluntary effort" and a strictly "quantifiable outcome" (B. Suits, K. Salen & E. Zimmerman). As long as participants have the ability to make decisions, the conditions of a game are satisfied. In modern development, games are frequently tested and played by fleets of automated agents or bots before their final release. These machines possess no voluntariness or desire to play, they are simply assigned to evaluate the system. Therefore, voluntary participation is not a strict requirement for a game system to exist. Furthermore, the necessity for a "quantifiable outcome" is excluded. While an "inequality of results" suggests a defined end, distinguishing winner from other players, modern simulation games often focus on exploring the encapsulated world rather than racking up points. In such systems, producing a universally measurable metric is difficult. One player might define victory as exploring 80% of the map, while another might define it as spotting the highest mountain. These two results are incomparable because they lack a common metric and scope, yet both occur within the bounds of a game.

According to the above definition, a high jump is not a game. But if that activity is held between several people, with a defined winning condition, a limited context, and an encapsulated system, it can be considered a game. For example, if a person conducts the "high jump" activity just for exercise purposes, it is just a normal activity. But if we define the encapsulated system: in the courtyard of the high school (boundary), only jumping one time (rule), without using a base (resource), where anyone who achieves the largest height that is not less than 40 centimeters wins (winning condition), the high jump becomes a game. And of course, with the said constraints, only one person can be the winner, resulting in unequal outcomes. Similarly, Sudoku is more like a puzzle than a game because it is suited for a single person with a pen or a pencil to play (or just a smartphone). It has its own set of rules, boundaries (only fill numbers inside the table), and of course, a winning condition (every square contains a number from 1 to 9, every row and column has 9 distinct numbers, etc.). But it does not have multiple participants competing, and it does not lead to an inequality of results (everybody who completes the quiz is considered a winner). However, to explicitly say the Sudoku is a puzzle, we mostly refer to the traditional edition (in magazines, newspapers, etc.) because some "cyberlized" versions of Sudoku have time constraints, leaderboard,... making it become a true game.

On the other hand, gambling is an interesting case. It does have its own rules (to define who the winner is, or more precisely, in which cases players get or lose money). It does have its boundaries (typically 1, 2, or 3 decks of cards). Lastly, it also has its specific winning condition. But if we look in the textbook, according to definitions given by R. Caillois or J. Huizinga, gambling is not a game because it is "serious," it connects to "material interest," and it is definitely not "unproductive." Some people just live a wealthy life by gambling (or at least, gamble with exceptional skills). Nevertheless, by my definition, gambling is a game. It is just a special case where a game can actually bring fortune or material interest, overriding the traditional requirement that a game must stand outside ordinary life.

As a final verdict, in the case where two machines log into a system and play a game with each other in a rule-respecting manner, we can safely call it a game. Even within textbook definitions, there were no strict distinctions explicitly forbidding machines in terms of participants or players. As long as the participants or players respect the rules of the game, remain within the game's boundaries, and aim to achieve the winning condition via legitimate methods, the game is being played. And if we recall the definition provided by C.C.Abt, these machines are capable of making decisions.  In history, there were many occasions when a human player competed against a machine or software (we call them bots or NPCs). These matches were still games, but instead of achieving some sort of entertainment purpose, they were conducted to evaluate the ability to challenge a human or maybe provide a report on how feasible the game was? (If the game was too difficult, it could hardly be accepted by common audiences).

Turning back to the initial definition of a game, throughout the history of the game industry, we are now witnessing games in various forms. So the definition of a game should be broad enough to accept even virtual games, board games, and digital sports. For example, one of the most notable games right now is _League of Legends_. The rule is very simple: 10 players are divided into 2 teams of 5, competing for a limited number of resources to achieve "ideal" states, and then trying to destroy the opponent's headquarters as soon as possible. The game is set in an encapsulated world that has its own set of physical laws, the boundary is the computer system as well (players' characters cannot suddenly jump out of the computer), and the result is definitely not equal because the game only ends when a headquarters is destroyed. Within the context of the game, players can eliminate other players' characters, facing no real-world consequences because it is contained entirely within a separate reality.

However, testing this definition against edge cases reveals where the boundaries of a "game" blur into other interactive software. The "sandbox" genre, for instance, challenges the necessity of a defined winning condition. In these titles, the player operates almost as a deity within a pocket dimension, able to manipulate the system, rules, and resources, yet still bounded by the software's parameters. Because there is no predefined victory state or inequality of results, a pure sandbox acts more as a digital toy than a formal game under this definition. Gamified applications present another controversy. Applications like Duolingo possess game-like characteristics such as an encapsulated context and victory states via leaderboards, but they do not enforce strict competition over limited resources. Resources in gamified apps are often limitless (refilled via waiting or purchasing), and the winning condition is entirely optional. Most importantly, gamification deliberately blurs the line between the encapsulated system and reality: the social connections and educational benefits directly impact the real world, violating the requirement that the game remains separate from our reality.

Applying this definition fundamentally shifts the role of the game designer. The textbook defines a game designer primarily as "an advocate for the player," someone who champions the user's experience, accessibility, and enjoyment throughout the development process. However, under this "system-creator" definition, the designer's primary responsibility becomes system architecture design. The designer must focus on engineering an encapsulated infrastructure with strict boundaries, ensuring that play activities happen safely, predictably, and in a highly controlled manner. Crafting an appealing world is not enough, the designer must carefully calculate and balance boundaries, rules, and resource economies to generate the necessary systemic pressure that drives player conflict to its peak. This architectural approach elevates the designer's role into one of engineering complex, modular systems. It poses more challenging, structural questions: How does one build an encapsulated context that affords players the freedom to compete, guarantees a defined victory state, and maintains structural integrity even if the participants are entirely automated? Furthermore, designers should take into account the consideration about "What if a player tries to exploit or breaking the internal logic to rule out the fairness of the game?".

In conclusion, defining a game as an encapsulated system with rules, boundaries and resources that results in an unequal victory state produces a resilient framework to analyze modern interactive media. It rules out the necessity of human participation, voluntariness, productivity and even emotional advocacy. Now, the game can be represented by measurable and structural mechanics. Additionally, with the evolution of the digital ecosystem, the game also blurs the distinction between human and machine participants. Thus, this definition ensures that we can safely evaluate the integrity of underlying architecture without the interference of "player's experience" - a concept that is too vague in some cases.
